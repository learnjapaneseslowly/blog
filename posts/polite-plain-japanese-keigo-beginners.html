Polite vs. Plain Japanese: A Beginner's Guide to Keigo

You have learned how to introduce yourself in Japanese, ask simple questions, and use basic particles. You can talk about everyday activities and understand simple sentences.

But as you start having more conversations, you may notice something interesting: Japanese speakers do not always speak in the same way.

Sometimes you hear sentences ending in です or ます. Other times, people use shorter forms that sound more casual. You may also encounter special expressions used when speaking respectfully to someone.

Why are there so many ways to say similar things?

Japanese has different levels of politeness, and choosing the right one helps you communicate naturally. The good news is that beginners only need a few basic patterns to get started.

In this guide, you will learn the difference between polite and plain Japanese, when to use each, and the three basic types of keigo (敬語). You will also find a simple 15-minute daily routine to practice these expressions.

1. What Is Polite Japanese? Understanding です and ます

Polite Japanese is often the best starting point for everyday conversations.

You will recognize it by two common sentence endings: です (desu) and ます (masu).

For example:

わたしは学生です。

Watashi wa gakusei desu.

I am a student.

毎日、日本語を勉強します。

Mainichi, nihongo o benkyou shimasu.

I study Japanese every day.

These forms create a polite tone without requiring complicated expressions.

You will commonly use polite Japanese when:

  • Introducing yourself to someone new.
  • Speaking with teachers or people you do not know well.
  • Asking questions in shops or restaurants.
  • Having conversations in a professional or formal setting.

For beginners, learning the です and ます patterns gives you a useful foundation.

Here are two more examples:

これは本です。

Kore wa hon desu.

This is a book.

水を飲みます。

Mizu o nomimasu.

I drink water.

You may already recognize these sentence patterns from your earlier grammar lessons. Now you are learning how the sentence endings affect the tone of your conversation.

2. What Is Plain Japanese? Speaking Naturally with Friends and Family

Plain Japanese is a more casual way of speaking. It is commonly used with close friends, family members, and people with whom you have an informal relationship.

You will also see plain forms in diaries, informal messages, and many written explanations.

Compare these sentences:

  • 学生です。 / 学生だ。 — I am a student.
  • 食べます。 / 食べる。 — I eat.
  • 行きます。 / 行く。 — I go.
  • 見ます。 / 見る。 — I watch.
  • おいしいです。 / おいしい。 — It is delicious.
  • 静かです。 / 静かだ。 — It is quiet.

Notice that plain forms are generally shorter.

For example:

Polite:

今日は暑いです。

Kyou wa atsui desu.

It is hot today.

Plain:

今日は暑い。

Kyou wa atsui.

It is hot today.

The meaning is essentially the same, but the tone is different.

An important point: plain Japanese is not automatically rude. It is perfectly natural when used with the right people in the right situations.

However, using plain speech with someone you have just met may sound too familiar. That is why beginners should first become comfortable with polite Japanese.

3. When Should You Use Polite or Plain Japanese?

Knowing both forms is useful, but knowing when to use them is even more important.

Think about the person you are speaking to and the situation you are in.

Here is a simple guide:

  • Meeting someone for the first time: Polite
  • Speaking with a teacher: Polite
  • Ordering at a restaurant: Polite
  • Talking with close friends: Plain
  • Talking with family: Usually plain
  • Writing a personal diary: Plain
  • Speaking in a professional setting: Polite or appropriate keigo

These are general guidelines, not strict rules. Relationships and circumstances can change how people speak.

For example, you might begin a conversation using polite Japanese when meeting someone for the first time. As you become closer, you may gradually start using plain Japanese.

This change is sometimes called tameguchi (タメ口), meaning casual or familiar speech.

You do not need to switch immediately. It is perfectly fine to continue using polite Japanese until you understand the relationship better.

Compare These Everyday Expressions

You may remember these useful expressions from your earlier speaking practice. Let's look at their polite and casual equivalents.

  • おはようございます。 / おはよう。 — Good morning.
  • ありがとうございます。 / ありがとう。 — Thank you.
  • すみません。 / ごめん。 — Sorry.
  • もう一度お願いします。 / もう一回言ってくれる？ — Could you say that again?

A small note about meaning: すみません can also mean “excuse me,” while ごめん is mainly used for apologies. They are not interchangeable in every situation.

The important lesson is that you can express similar intentions in different ways depending on how formal or familiar the conversation is.

Practice saying both versions aloud. This will help you recognize the difference when listening to native Japanese speakers.

4. The Basics of Keigo: Three Types of Respectful Language

You may have heard the word keigo (敬語).

Keigo is the Japanese system of respectful and polite language. It is especially important in professional situations and when communicating with people who should be treated respectfully.

For beginners, there are three main categories to recognize.

You do not need to memorize complicated rules yet. Start by understanding what each category does.

Sonkeigo: Respectful Language

Sonkeigo (尊敬語) is used to show respect toward another person's actions or status.

For example:

先生が来ます。

Sensei ga kimasu.

The teacher is coming.

A more respectful version is:

先生がいらっしゃいます。

Sensei ga irasshaimasu.

The teacher is coming.

The word いらっしゃいます is a respectful expression associated with 行く (go), 来る (come), and いる (be).

You do not need to master every special verb now. Simply recognize that sonkeigo raises the person you are talking about.

Kenjougo: Humble Language

Kenjougo (謙譲語) is used to speak humbly about your own actions in relation to another person.

For example:

明日、先生のところへ行きます。

Ashita, sensei no tokoro e ikimasu.

I will go to the teacher's place tomorrow.

A more humble version is:

明日、先生のところへ伺います。

Ashita, sensei no tokoro e ukagaimasu.

I will visit the teacher tomorrow.

The word 伺います is a humble expression used for going to or visiting someone.

The basic idea is simple: sonkeigo shows respect toward the other person, while kenjougo makes your own actions sound humble.

Teineigo: Polite Language

Teineigo (丁寧語) is the polite language you have already started learning.

It commonly uses です and ます.

For example:

食べる → 食べます (eat)

行く → 行きます (go)

学生だ → 学生です (am a student)

You can think of teineigo as the everyday polite style that helps you communicate respectfully without using advanced keigo.

For now, focus on recognizing these three categories. You will have plenty of time to learn more advanced expressions later.

5. Three Common Mistakes Beginners Make

Learning different levels of politeness takes time. Here are three mistakes to avoid.

Mistake 1: Using plain Japanese with everyone

Plain Japanese is natural with friends and family, but it may sound too familiar when speaking with teachers, strangers, or people in professional situations.

When you are unsure, start with polite Japanese.

Mistake 2: Mixing polite and plain styles without understanding the context

For example, suddenly switching between です・ます and casual sentence endings in a short conversation may make your speech sound inconsistent.

Try to maintain one style within a conversation unless there is a reason to change.

Mistake 3: Thinking that keigo is always necessary

You do not need complicated honorific expressions in every conversation.

A simple ありがとうございます or すみません is often perfectly appropriate.

Start with everyday polite Japanese, and learn keigo gradually as you encounter situations where it is useful.

6. A 7-Day Japanese Politeness Study Plan

You can learn these patterns without spending hours studying grammar.

Try this simple 15-minute routine, focusing on one everyday situation each day.

  • Day 1 — Polite introductions: Practice introducing yourself using です.
  • Day 2 — Plain introductions: Introduce yourself again using casual forms.
  • Day 3 — Everyday expressions: Compare おはようございます and おはよう, and other useful pairs.
  • Day 4 — Shopping and restaurants: Practice polite requests and questions.
  • Day 5 — Basic keigo: Recognize sonkeigo, kenjougo, and teineigo.
  • Day 6 — Switching styles: Create short conversations with friends and teachers.
  • Day 7 — Review: Repeat your examples without looking at your notes.

Use this structure every day:

Minutes 1–5: Review yesterday's expressions.

Minutes 6–10: Learn the day's topic and read three examples aloud.

Minutes 11–15: Create your own short conversation.

Keep the examples simple and connected to your real life.

For instance, imagine greeting a friend in the morning, then greeting a teacher later that day. Practice changing your expression to match the situation.

7. How to Remember What You Learn

Learning a grammar pattern once is not enough. You need to revisit it regularly.

A simple spaced-repetition schedule can help:

  • The next day: Review the expressions you learned.
  • Three days later: Test yourself without looking at the examples.
  • One week later: Create new sentences using the same patterns.

You can keep a small notebook with three columns:

  • Japanese expression.
  • Polite or plain version.
  • Situation where you would use it.

For example, write おはようございます and おはよう next to each other, then note that the first is more polite and the second is commonly used with friends and family.

Over time, you will start recognizing which expressions sound natural in different situations.

What's Next? Build Your Japanese Conversation Skills

Understanding polite and plain Japanese is an important step toward having more natural conversations.

You do not need to master all of keigo immediately. Start with です and ます, learn common plain forms, and gradually become familiar with respectful expressions.

Remember: the goal is not simply to speak formally. It is to choose language that fits the person and situation.
