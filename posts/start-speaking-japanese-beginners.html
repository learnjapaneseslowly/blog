How to Start Speaking Japanese When You're Afraid of Making Mistakes

You may understand more Japanese than you can say. You recognize greetings and remember useful words, but when it is time to speak, your mind goes blank. You worry about choosing the wrong word or mispronouncing a sound.

That feeling is normal. Speaking is a skill, not a test of how much you have memorized. You do not need perfect grammar or a large vocabulary. You need a small routine that makes speaking feel familiar.

For now, aim to speak a little Japanese every day, even when you are alone. 15 focused minutes can build a useful habit.

1. Make Your First Goal Small

You do not need to start with a five-minute conversation. Choose one small goal: say your name, describe the weather, or order an item in a pretend shop.

A small goal makes progress easier to notice. Instead of asking, "Can I speak Japanese?", ask, "Can I say three sentences about myself?" That is a goal you can practice.

Use notes at first if you need them. Read a sentence aloud, look away, and try again. Gradually reduce your support.

2. Try Speaking to Yourself

Talking to yourself may feel strange, but it lets you practice without pressure. You can pause, repeat, or start over whenever you like.

Begin with a short self-introduction:

わたしは [name] です。 (Watashi wa [name] desu.) — I am [name].

[place] に すんでいます。 ([Place] ni sunde imasu.) — I live in [place].

日本語を べんきょうしています。 (Nihongo o benkyō shite imasu.) — I am studying Japanese.

Narrate what you are doing: "I am drinking water," "This is my bag," or "It is sunny today." If you do not know a word, leave a gap, look it up after practice, and keep going. Do not let a pause end the session.

Reuse familiar sentence patterns. Repetition helps useful language become easier to retrieve.

3. Use Shadowing to Find Your Rhythm

Shadowing means repeating a short piece of spoken Japanese just after the speaker. It helps you notice rhythm, pauses, and complete phrases.

For a beginner, keep the audio very short. A greeting or a single sentence is enough.

  • Listen once without speaking. Notice the overall sound.
  • Listen again and read the Japanese text, if a transcript is available.
  • Pause after each short phrase and repeat it aloud.
  • Play it again and speak along with the audio.
  • Repeat once more, focusing on rhythm rather than speed.

Choose material you can mostly understand. If it is too fast, slow it down or practice one phrase at a time. Do not force perfect imitation. Aim to feel more comfortable hearing and producing Japanese.

If listening practice is also part of your routine, read How to Start Listening to Japanese When You Understand Almost Nothing. Listening and speaking support each other: the more familiar a phrase sounds, the easier it is to try saying it.

4. Learn a Few Phrases You Can Use Immediately

Memorize phrases as whole units before worrying about every grammar detail. Say each one slowly, then at a natural pace. The romaji readings are there to help you pronounce each phrase. Over time, try to recognize the Japanese text itself.

おはようございます。Ohayō gozaimasu.Good morning.
こんにちは。Konnichiwa.Hello.
ありがとうございます。Arigatō gozaimasu.Thank you.
すみません。Sumimasen.Excuse me. / I'm sorry.
はじめまして。Hajimemashite.Nice to meet you.
わたしは [name] です。Watashi wa [name] desu.I am [name].
よろしくお願いします。Yoroshiku onegaishimasu.A polite set phrase used when meeting someone. It has no exact English equivalent; "Nice to meet you" and "Please take care of me" both come close.
これをください。Kore o kudasai.This one, please.
いくらですか。Ikura desu ka?How much is it?
もう一度お願いします。Mō ichido onegaishimasu.One more time, please.

Practice two or three phrases daily. Repeat while reading, cover the romaji, and try again from the Japanese. Then imagine a situation for each phrase: say すみません before asking a shop assistant a question, or もう一度お願いします when you need someone to repeat.

Pronunciation note: は is read "wa" when it is used as a particle, as in わたしは, and in こんにちは. を is always read "o" in modern Japanese, as in これをください. Notice these patterns as you practice. You do not need to master them all today.

5. Follow a 15-Minute Speaking Routine

Use the same structure each day. A familiar routine makes it easier to begin.

  • Minutes 1–2: Warm up. Say おはようございます, こんにちは, or ありがとうございます three times. Relax your jaw and speak clearly.
  • Minutes 3–5: Shadow. Repeat one short line from beginner audio. Listen, pause, copy, and then speak along.
  • Minutes 6–10: Talk about yourself or your surroundings. Say three to five simple sentences. You might introduce yourself, describe breakfast, or name objects on your desk.
  • Minutes 11–13: Practice useful phrases. Choose two phrases from the table. Say each one from memory and use it in a tiny pretend conversation.
  • Minutes 14–15: Review. Record yourself saying one sentence, or write down what felt difficult. Choose one thing to repeat tomorrow.

Pauses are part of learning. If you miss a day, return to the routine the next day instead of trying to catch up with a long session.

6. Let Mistakes Be Part of the Practice

When you are afraid of mistakes, it is tempting to wait until you feel ready. But confidence usually comes after repeated attempts, not before them.

Treat each attempt as information. Shorten a difficult sentence, use a simpler word, or listen and repeat if the pronunciation feels unclear. Every attempt shows you what to practice next.

It can help to separate practice from performance. During your private 15 minutes, allow yourself to experiment. When you eventually speak with another person, focus on communicating one idea rather than producing a perfect sentence. A patient listener can often understand more than you expect, and asking someone to repeat or speak slowly is a useful communication skill.

If you are still learning the Japanese writing systems, keep building that foundation too. How to Learn Hiragana and Katakana in One Week can help you plan your first steps. You can use romaji as a temporary pronunciation aid, but learning to recognize kana will make beginner materials easier to use.

Start With One Sentence Today

Choose one phrase and say it aloud three times. Tomorrow, add another. A week of short sessions gives you practice introducing yourself, describing familiar things, and using everyday phrases.

You do not need to wait until you feel confident. Start small and let repetition do its work. One imperfect sentence today is more useful than a perfect sentence you never try.
