How to Start Learning Kanji as a Beginner: A Simple 15-Minute Daily Plan

Introduction: Kanji Doesn't Have to Be Scary

If you're just starting to learn Japanese, kanji may seem like the biggest challenge. Some characters look simple, while others have many strokes. You may also notice that one character can have more than one pronunciation.

It's easy to feel overwhelmed, but you don't need to memorize hundreds of characters right away.

A better approach is to start small. Learn a few useful kanji, understand how they work, and review them regularly. Even 15 minutes a day can help you build a foundation.

In this guide, you'll learn what kanji are, how to understand their basic components, and why they have different readings. You'll also meet 10 beginner-friendly kanji and follow a gentle seven-day study plan.

The goal isn't to master kanji in one week. It's to make your first steps feel manageable.

What Is Kanji?

Kanji are characters originally developed for writing Chinese and later adopted to write Japanese. Unlike hiragana and katakana, which mainly represent sounds, kanji often carry meaning.

For example:

  • 山 means mountain.
  • 川 means river.
  • 人 means person.

Kanji are commonly combined with other kanji or hiragana to form Japanese words and sentences.

You may already know that Japanese uses three writing systems:

  • Hiragana: Used for grammatical endings, particles, and many Japanese words.
  • Katakana: Often used for foreign loanwords and names.
  • Kanji: Used to represent many words and meaningful parts of words.

If you haven't learned hiragana and katakana yet, start with those first. They will make your kanji journey much easier.

Understanding Radicals and Kanji Components

One helpful way to study kanji is to look at their components instead of treating every character as a completely new picture.

Many kanji contain smaller elements that can provide clues about meaning or pronunciation. Some of these elements are called radicals.

A radical is a component used to classify kanji in dictionaries. Some radicals also give useful hints about a character's meaning.

For example:

  • 氵 is the water-related component found in characters such as 海 (sea) and 河 (river).
  • 木 means tree or wood and appears in characters such as 林 (woods) and 森 (forest).
  • 亻 is a person-related component found in characters such as 休 (rest).

These clues aren't perfect rules. A component doesn't always tell you exactly what a kanji means or how to pronounce it. However, noticing familiar parts can make unfamiliar characters easier to remember.

Try asking yourself, "Have I seen this shape before?" That small habit can make kanji feel less random.

On'yomi and Kun'yomi: Why Kanji Have Different Readings

One of the most confusing things about kanji is that a single character can have several readings.

There are two broad categories:

  • On'yomi (音読み) is a reading based on the Chinese pronunciation of a character when it was borrowed into Japanese.
  • Kun'yomi (訓読み) is a Japanese reading associated with the character's meaning.

For example, the kanji 山 means mountain.

  • On'yomi: さん (san), as in 富士山 (ふじさん, Fujisan).
  • Kun'yomi: やま (yama), as in 山 (やま, mountain).

Another example is 人, meaning person.

  • On'yomi: じん (jin), as in 日本人 (にほんじん, Japanese person).
  • Kun'yomi: ひと (hito), as in 人 (ひと, person).

You don't need to memorize every possible reading when you first encounter a kanji.

Instead, learn the character together with a useful word. This gives you a real context for remembering its pronunciation.

A Gentle 7-Day, 15-Minute Study Plan

You don't need a long study session to begin learning kanji. Try this simple routine: spend five minutes learning new characters, five minutes writing them, and five minutes reviewing.

The plan below introduces 10 characters gradually. Some days include only one or two new characters so you have enough time to review.

Day 1: 日 and 月

Learn 日 (sun/day) and 月 (moon/month).

  • 5 minutes: Look at each character and learn its meaning.
  • 5 minutes: Practice writing each one, following the correct stroke order.
  • 5 minutes: Cover the meanings and test yourself.

Try connecting 日 with the sun and 月 with the moon. Visual associations can help you remember them.

Day 2: 人 and 山

Today, add 人 (person) and 山 (mountain).

  • 5 minutes: Study the two new characters.
  • 5 minutes: Write each character several times.
  • 5 minutes: Review all four characters from Days 1 and 2.

Say the meanings aloud as you look at each character.

Day 3: 川 and 水

Learn 川 (river) and 水 (water).

  • 5 minutes: Study the characters and their meanings.
  • 5 minutes: Practice writing them.
  • 5 minutes: Shuffle your six kanji and identify each one.

Try to notice the visual differences between 川 and 水 rather than relying on their positions in a list.

Day 4: 火 and 木

Add 火 (fire) and 木 (tree/wood).

  • 5 minutes: Learn the two new characters.
  • 5 minutes: Write them while checking stroke order.
  • 5 minutes: Review all eight characters.

If you like, draw a small picture beside each character in your notebook. It can make the meaning easier to recall.

Day 5: 大 and 小

Today, learn 大 (big) and 小 (small).

  • 5 minutes: Study both characters.
  • 5 minutes: Practice writing them.
  • 5 minutes: Review all 10 kanji learned so far.

Notice how 大 and 小 express opposite ideas. Connecting related meanings can make vocabulary easier to remember.

Day 6: Learn Words, Not Just Characters

Today, focus on using your 10 kanji in simple vocabulary.

Choose a few examples:

  • 山 (やま) — mountain
  • 川 (かわ) — river
  • 水 (みず) — water
  • 木 (き) — tree
  • 大きい (おおきい) — big
  • 小さい (ちいさい) — small

Spend five minutes reading the words, five minutes writing them, and five minutes testing yourself.

Day 7: Review and Test Yourself

Use the final day to check what you remember.

  • 5 minutes: Look at all 10 kanji and recall their meanings.
  • 5 minutes: Write as many as you can without looking.
  • 5 minutes: Read the example words and check their pronunciations.

Don't worry if you forget a few. Mark the difficult characters and return to them during next week's practice.

Why Stroke Order Matters

When learning kanji, it's tempting to copy a character until it looks familiar. But paying attention to stroke order is also important.

Stroke order refers to the sequence in which you write the individual strokes of a character.

Following standard stroke order can help you:

  • Write characters more consistently.
  • Understand how their components fit together.
  • Build a smoother writing habit.
  • Read handwritten characters more comfortably over time.

A common beginner guideline is to write from top to bottom and from left to right. However, kanji have more specific stroke-order conventions, so use a reliable stroke-order chart or dictionary when you're unsure.

Don't focus on writing quickly. Start slowly, follow the correct sequence, and aim for clear, balanced characters.

Three Tips for Remembering Kanji

1. Learn Kanji With Vocabulary

A character by itself can feel abstract. A word gives it context.

Instead of memorizing only 山, learn 山 (やま), meaning mountain. When you meet 富士山 (ふじさん), you'll see the same character used in a different word and reading.

2. Review a Little Every Day

Short, regular reviews help you notice which characters you remember and which ones need more practice.

Before learning new kanji, spend a few minutes reviewing yesterday's characters. You can use paper flashcards or a simple study notebook.

3. Don't Memorize Every Reading at Once

A kanji may have several readings, but beginners don't need to learn them all immediately.

Start with one useful word for each character. Add other readings when you encounter them in vocabulary.

This keeps your study sessions focused and prevents unnecessary frustration.

What to Learn Next

After your first week, continue with a small number of new kanji each day. Three to five characters can be a reasonable target when you have time to review them, but it's perfectly fine to learn fewer.

Gradually explore common kanji combinations, everyday vocabulary, and the ways kanji work with hiragana.

Remember that learning Japanese is a long-term journey. You don't need to rush through hundreds of characters. Build a steady routine, celebrate what you recognize, and return to the characters you find difficult.
