Japanese Verb and Adjective Conjugation: A Beginner's Guide

You have learned basic Japanese vocabulary, particles, and simple sentence patterns. You can introduce yourself, talk about everyday activities, and recognize polite and casual expressions.

Now it is time to learn another important part of Japanese grammar: conjugation.

You may have noticed that Japanese verbs and adjectives change their endings depending on what you want to say. For example, 食べます (tabemasu) can become 食べて (tabete), 食べた (tabeta), or 食べない (tabenai).

Why do these changes happen?

The good news is that Japanese conjugation follows patterns. Once you understand the three verb groups and a few basic rules, you can create many useful sentences.

In this guide, you will learn the basics of Japanese verb and adjective conjugation, starting with the familiar ます-form. You will also find a simple 15-minute daily routine to help you practice without feeling overwhelmed.

1. What Is Japanese Conjugation?

Conjugation means changing a word's form to express different grammatical meanings.

In Japanese, verbs can change to express things such as:

  • Politeness
  • Past actions
  • Negative actions
  • Connections between actions

For example, look at the verb 食べる (taberu), meaning "to eat."

JapaneseReadingMeaning
食べますtabemasuEat (polite)
食べてtabeteConnecting form
食べたtabetaAte
食べないtabenaiDo not eat

The basic meaning remains connected to eating, but the ending changes how the verb works in a sentence.

Adjectives also change their forms. For example, 高い (takai), meaning "expensive" or "high," can become 高くない (takakunai), meaning "not expensive."

Understanding these patterns will help you build more natural Japanese sentences.

2. The Three Japanese Verb Groups

Before learning conjugation rules, you need to know that Japanese verbs belong to three main groups.

Each group follows different patterns.

Group 1: U-Verbs (Godan Verbs)

Group 1 verbs are often called u-verbs because their dictionary forms end in a sound from the Japanese u-row.

Examples include:

Dictionary formMeaningます-form
書く (kaku)Write書きます
飲む (nomu)Drink飲みます
話す (hanasu)Speak話します
買う (kau)Buy買います
待つ (matsu)Wait待ちます

These verbs change their final sounds depending on the conjugation pattern.

Group 2: Ru-Verbs (Ichidan Verbs)

Group 2 verbs usually end in る (ru) and often have an い or え sound before it.

Examples:

Dictionary formMeaningます-form
食べる (taberu)Eat食べます
見る (miru)See / Watch見ます
起きる (okiru)Wake up起きます
寝る (neru)Sleep寝ます

These verbs are generally easier to conjugate. You often remove る and add the appropriate ending.

One important warning: not every verb ending in る belongs to Group 2. For example, 帰る (kaeru), meaning "to return," is a Group 1 verb. Learn common exceptions as individual vocabulary items.

Group 3: Irregular Verbs

Group 3 contains two basic irregular verbs:

Dictionary formMeaningます-form
する (suru)Doします
来る (kuru)Come来ます (kimasu)

These verbs do not follow the regular Group 1 or Group 2 patterns, so memorize their basic forms.

Many verbs ending in する follow the same pattern. For example, 勉強する (benkyou suru), meaning "to study," becomes 勉強します (benkyou shimasu).

3. Why Start with the ます-Form?

If you have already studied polite Japanese, you probably recognize ます.

It is a useful starting point because you can use it in everyday polite conversations while learning other forms.

For example:

書きます → 書く

食べます → 食べる

します → する

来ます → 来る

Learn these pairs together. The ます-form gives you a familiar reference, while the dictionary form helps you identify the verb group and apply other conjugation rules.

One important point: you cannot simply replace ます with て or た. Different verb groups require different changes.

For example:

書きます → 書いて

食べます → 食べて

します → して

Start by recognizing the relationship between the polite and dictionary forms. Then practice one conjugation pattern at a time.

4. How to Make the て-Form

The て-form is one of the most useful Japanese verb forms.

It helps connect actions and creates everyday expressions.

For example:

食べてください。

Tabete kudasai.

Please eat.

勉強しています。

Benkyou shite imasu.

I am studying.

Let's look at the basic rules.

Group 2: Remove る and Add て

This is the simplest pattern.

食べる → 食べて

見る → 見て

寝る → 寝て

Group 3: Memorize the Irregular Forms

する → して

来る → 来て (kite)

Group 1: Follow the Ending Patterns

Group 1 verbs require a little more attention.

Dictionary endingて-form endingExample
う・つ・るって買う → 買って
む・ぶ・ぬんで飲む → 飲んで
くいて書く → 書いて
ぐいで泳ぐ → 泳いで
すして話す → 話して

There is one important beginner exception:

行く (iku) → 行って (itte)

Although 行く ends in く, its て-form is 行って, not 行いて.

Memorize this common verb separately.

For now, practice these patterns with familiar verbs instead of trying to learn dozens of new words.

5. Understanding the た-Form and ない-Form

After learning the て-form, the た-form becomes easier.

The た-Form: Talking About the Past

The た-form expresses a completed action in the past.

For basic practice, use the same sound-change patterns as the て-form:

て becomes た.

で becomes だ.

Examples:

Dictionary formて-formた-form
書く書いて書いた
飲む飲んで飲んだ
食べる食べて食べた
話す話して話した
するしてした
来る来て来た

For example:

昨日、本を読みました。

Kinou, hon o yomimashita.

I read a book yesterday.

The polite past form is useful in everyday conversations. The plain past form, such as 読んだ (yonda), is useful when practicing casual Japanese.

The ない-Form: Making Negative Sentences

The ない-form expresses that something does not happen or is not done.

For Group 2 verbs, remove る and add ない:

食べる → 食べない

見る → 見ない

寝る → 寝ない

For Group 1 verbs, change the final u sound to its corresponding a-row sound and add ない.

Examples:

書く → 書かない

飲む → 飲まない

話す → 話さない

買う → 買わない

Notice that う changes to わ, as in 買わない.

The irregular verbs are:

する → しない

来る → 来ない (konai)

A simple example:

今日はコーヒーを飲まない。

Kyou wa koohii o nomanai.

I will not drink coffee today.

You can also use polite negative forms with ません, such as 飲みません. For now, focus on recognizing the basic plain ない-form.

6. Basic い-Adjective and な-Adjective Conjugation

Japanese adjectives also have different types.

The two basic categories are い-adjectives and な-adjectives.

い-Adjectives

These adjectives typically end in い.

For example:

高い (takai) — Expensive / High

寒い (samui) — Cold

おいしい (oishii) — Delicious

Use 高い as an example:

FormJapaneseMeaning
Basic高いExpensive
Negative高くないNot expensive
Past高かったWas expensive
て-form高くてExpensive and...

For polite sentences, you can add です:

高いです — It is expensive.

高くないです — It is not expensive.

高かったです — It was expensive.

Remember that いい (good) is a common exception. Its negative and past forms use よく:

いい → よくない

いい → よかった

な-Adjectives

な-adjectives behave differently.

For example:

静か (shizuka) — Quiet

便利 (benri) — Convenient

元気 (genki) — Healthy / Energetic

Use 静か as an example:

FormJapaneseMeaning
Basic polite静かですIt is quiet.
Negative静かじゃないですIt is not quiet.
Past静かでしたIt was quiet.
て-form静かでQuiet and...

When a な-adjective comes directly before a noun, add な.

For example:

静かな部屋

Shizuka na heya

A quiet room.

A useful warning: きれい (kirei), meaning "beautiful" or "clean," ends in い but is a な-adjective. You say きれいな花, not きれい花.

Learn whether each adjective belongs to the い or な group rather than relying only on its final sound.

7. Three Common Conjugation Mistakes

Here are three mistakes to watch for as you practice.

Mistake 1: Assuming every verb ending in る is Group 2

帰る is a Group 1 verb. Its て-form is 帰って, not 帰て.

Mistake 2: Adding だった to every adjective

For an い-adjective, 高い becomes 高かった, not 高いだった.

Remember that い-adjectives change their own endings.

Mistake 3: Trying to learn every form at once

It is tempting to memorize all conjugation tables in one session. However, this often makes the patterns harder to remember.

Start with the ます-form, then learn て-form, た-form, and ない-form separately.

Small, repeated practice is more useful than rushing through everything.

8. A 7-Day Japanese Conjugation Study Plan

You do not need a long study session to understand these patterns.

Try this simple plan, spending approximately 15 minutes each day.

DayFocusPractice
Day 1Verb groupsIdentify Group 1, 2, and 3 using familiar verbs.
Day 2ます-form and dictionary formPractice matching five verb pairs.
Day 3て-formConjugate verbs from all three groups.
Day 4て-form in sentencesPractice 〜てください and 〜ています.
Day 5た-form and ない-formCreate past and negative sentences.
Day 6い-adjectivesPractice negative, past, and て-forms.
Day 7な-adjectives and reviewPractice both adjective groups and review everything.

Use the same structure each day:

  • Minutes 1–5: Review yesterday's examples.
  • Minutes 6–10: Learn one new form and practice three to five examples.
  • Minutes 11–15: Write your own sentences and read them aloud.

For example, describe what you ate yesterday, what you are doing today, and what you do not want to eat.

These familiar situations make grammar easier to remember.

9. How to Remember What You Learn

Conjugation requires repetition. You may understand a rule today but forget it a few days later.

A simple spaced-repetition routine can help:

  • The next day: Review the forms you learned.
  • Three days later: Test yourself without looking at your notes.
  • One week later: Create new sentences using the same patterns.

Keep a small grammar notebook with three columns:

  • Dictionary form.
  • Conjugated form.
  • Example sentence.

For instance:

書く → 書いて → 名前を書いてください。

Kaku → Kaite → Namae o kaite kudasai.

Write your name, please.

This helps connect the conjugation rule to a real expression rather than memorizing isolated endings.

What's Next? Build Your Japanese Grammar Step by Step

Japanese conjugation may seem complicated at first, but the patterns become easier once you recognize the three verb groups.

Start with the ます-form you already know. Practice the て-form, then add the た-form and ない-form. After that, work on the two adjective groups.

You do not need to learn everything perfectly in one week. The goal is to understand one pattern at a time and gradually use it in everyday sentences.
