Japanese Question Words: A Beginner's Guide to Asking Questions

You have learned how to introduce yourself, talk about everyday activities, and describe things in Japanese.

But conversations are not just about telling people things. You also need to ask questions!

How do you ask someone where they live? How can you find out what time a shop opens? And how do you start a friendly conversation with a new Japanese friend?

Japanese questions are easier than you might think. You only need to understand a few basic patterns and six useful question words.

In this guide, you will discover how to use the question marker か, learn six essential Japanese question words, and practice asking simple, polite questions you can use right away.

1. What Is a Japanese Question Sentence?

A question sentence is a sentence used to ask someone for information or confirmation.

In Japanese, you can often turn a simple statement into a question by adding か (ka) at the end.

For example:

これは本です。
Kore wa hon desu.
This is a book.

これは本ですか。
Kore wa hon desu ka.
Is this a book?

Notice that the sentence structure stays almost the same. You simply add か to make it a polite question.

This is one of the easiest ways to start asking questions in Japanese.

2. How to Use か at the End of a Sentence

The particle か is commonly used at the end of polite Japanese questions.

For example:

  • 学生ですか。(Gakusei desu ka.) — Are you a student?
  • コーヒーを飲みますか。(Koohii o nomimasu ka.) — Do you drink coffee?
  • 明日、行きますか。(Ashita, ikimasu ka.) — Are you going tomorrow?

These questions can usually be answered with:

  • はい (Hai) — Yes.
  • いいえ (Iie) — No.

For example:

A: コーヒーを飲みますか。
Do you drink coffee?

B: はい、飲みます。
Yes, I do.

You may also hear casual questions without か. In everyday conversations with friends, speakers often use a rising intonation instead of か.

For now, beginners can focus on the polite ですか and ますか patterns.

3. Six Essential Japanese Question Words

Learning a few question words will allow you to ask many different questions.

Here are six essential words to remember.

JapaneseReadingMeaning
何なに (nani)What
どこdokoWhere
いつitsuWhen
誰dareWho
なぜnazeWhy
どうdouHow

Let's look at how to use each one.

What — 何 (なに)

Use なに when asking about something or asking someone to identify an object.

これは何ですか。
Kore wa nan desu ka.
What is this?

何を食べますか。
Nani o tabemasu ka.
What will you eat?

These are useful questions when shopping, eating out, or learning new vocabulary.

Where — どこ

Use どこ to ask about a place or location.

駅はどこですか。
Eki wa doko desu ka.
Where is the station?

どこに住んでいますか。
Doko ni sunde imasu ka.
Where do you live?

When — いつ

Use いつ to ask about time or when something happens.

いつ日本へ行きますか。
Itsu Nihon e ikimasu ka.
When are you going to Japan?

いつ勉強しますか。
Itsu benkyou shimasu ka.
When do you study?

Who — 誰 (だれ)

Use だれ to ask about a person.

誰と行きますか。
Dare to ikimasu ka.
Who are you going with?

あの人は誰ですか。
Ano hito wa dare desu ka.
Who is that person?

Why — なぜ

Use なぜ to ask for a reason.

なぜ日本語を勉強しますか。
Naze nihongo o benkyou shimasu ka.
Why do you study Japanese?

You may also hear どうして (doushite), which is another common way to ask why.

How — どう

Use どう to ask about the condition, quality, or impression of something.

日本語の勉強はどうですか。
Nihongo no benkyou wa dou desu ka.
How is your Japanese study going?

You can also use どうやって (dou yatte) to ask about a method.

どうやって学校へ行きますか。
Dou yatte gakkou e ikimasu ka.
How do you get to school?

4. Asking Questions with Where, When, and Who

Some question words work naturally with particles you have already learned.

For example, look at these three questions:

  • どこで食べますか。 — Where will you eat?
  • いつ学校へ行きますか。 — When will you go to school?
  • 誰と映画を見ますか。 — Who will you watch a movie with?

Notice how で, へ, and と appear in familiar positions.

You do not need to change the entire sentence structure when adding a question word. Simply replace the missing information with a question word.

This is a useful way to build new questions from sentences you already understand.

5. Asking Questions with Why and How

Why and how questions are useful when you want more than a simple yes-or-no answer.

For example:

どうして日本語を勉強していますか。
Why are you studying Japanese?

どうやって料理を作りますか。
How do you make this dish?

Try answering these questions about yourself.

You can use simple expressions such as:

  • 日本が好きだからです。 — Because I like Japan.
  • 電車で行きます。 — I go by train.
  • 毎日勉強します。 — I study every day.

Even short answers help you practice connecting questions with complete sentences.

6. How to Ask Questions Politely

When speaking with someone you do not know well, polite questions are a good starting point.

You can use expressions such as:

すみません、駅はどこですか。
Excuse me, where is the station?

これは何ですか。
What is this?

もう一度お願いします。
One more time, please.

Adding すみません (sumimasen) before a question can make it sound more considerate when getting someone's attention.

You can also use ください (kudasai) when making a simple request.

For example:

ゆっくり話してください。
Please speak slowly.

You do not need complicated expressions to ask useful everyday questions. Start with simple, polite phrases and add more as you gain confidence.

7. Three Common Mistakes Beginners Make

Mistake 1: Forgetting か in polite yes-or-no questions

When using the basic polite question pattern, remember to add か at the end.

Mistake 2: Moving question words to the beginning automatically

Japanese question words usually stay in the position where the missing information would normally appear.

For example, 何を食べますか is natural. You do not need to rearrange the sentence like an English question.

Mistake 3: Confusing どう and どうやって

どう asks how something is or what something is like. どうやって asks how to do something or by what method.

Practice these expressions in separate sentences to remember the difference.

8. A 7-Day Japanese Question Practice Plan

You can learn these question patterns in small steps with just 15 minutes of practice each day.

DayFocusPractice
Day 1かMake five yes-or-no questions.
Day 2なにAsk about food and everyday objects.
Day 3どこAsk about places and locations.
Day 4いつAsk about daily activities and plans.
Day 5だれAsk about people and friends.
Day 6なぜAsk about reasons and practice simple answers.
Day 7どうAsk about experiences and methods, then review all six question words.

Use this simple routine every day:

  • Minutes 1–5: Review yesterday's questions.
  • Minutes 6–10: Learn the new question word and create three examples.
  • Minutes 11–15: Ask and answer the questions aloud.

Try using questions about your own daily life. This makes the practice more meaningful and easier to remember.

9. How to Review and Remember Your Questions

Learning question words once is not enough. You need to use them regularly.

Try this simple spaced-repetition schedule:

  • The next day: Review the questions you practiced.
  • Three days later: Test yourself without looking at your notes.
  • One week later: Create new questions using the same words.

You can keep a small notebook with three columns:

  • Japanese question.
  • English meaning.
  • Your own answer.

For example:

いつ日本語を勉強しますか。
When do you study Japanese?

毎晩勉強します。
I study every evening.

This approach helps you practice both asking and answering questions.

What's Next? Start Having More Natural Japanese Conversations

Asking questions is an important step toward having real conversations in Japanese.

Start with か and the six essential question words. Practice one pattern at a time, use familiar vocabulary, and try asking real questions in your daily life.

You do not need perfect grammar to start communicating. A few simple questions can already help you learn more, connect with people, and enjoy Japanese more.
